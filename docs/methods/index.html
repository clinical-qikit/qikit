    Methods: funnel plots and over-dispersion¶

    This is qikit's record of how its funnel plots are computed, which published method each step follows, and where it deliberately departs from that method and why. The numbered references at the end are the ones cited, as [n], in the docstrings of src/qikit/funnel.py and src/qikit/_funnel/, the core modules they call (core/contours.py, core/dispersion.py, core/pvalues.py, core/power.py, core/dist.py), and the TypeScript twin (excel-addin/packages/engine/src/stats.ts, spc-core.ts).

    Time-ordered control charts (qic(): run, I, p, u, Laney p′/u′ and the rest) are recorded in qic-methods.md, with their conformance to qicharts2.

    1. Which charts are funnels¶

    Funnels are drawn by funnel(). Its measure= and overdispersion= pick one of six engine charts, whose codes the engine's docstrings and the shared fixtures use:

    funnel(...) Engine chart Count model Limits Over-dispersion
    measure="proportion", overdispersion=False p Binomial Exact binomial [1, A.1.1] Estimated and reported, not applied
    measure="rate", overdispersion=False u Poisson Exact Poisson, exposure as denominator [1, A.2.1, A.2.3] Estimated and reported, not applied
    measure="oe", overdispersion=False oe Poisson Exact Poisson on expected events [1, A.2.1] Estimated and reported, not applied
    measure="proportion" (default overdispersion=True) pp Beta-binomial Exact quantiles of the additive model [1, eq. 11] Additive τ² [1, §5], applied
    measure="rate" up Poisson–gamma Exact quantiles of the additive model [1, eq. 11; 7] Additive τ² [1, §5], applied
    measure="oe" oep Poisson–gamma Exact quantiles of the additive model [1, eq. 11; 7] Additive τ² [1, §5], applied

    The contours are 99.8% and 95% by default (limits=). within_dispersion= scales any count model for counts that clump within cases (§4.12), and the binomial for the items of one survey (§4.13). qic() keeps p/u with 3σ Shewhart limits and pp/up with Laney's σ_z [6] for time order; it no longer draws funnels.

    2. What each chart answers¶

    The two kinds of funnel ask different questions, and both are legitimate:

    • Unadjusted (p, u and oe): is this provider's result consistent with the target, given chance (and, for O/E, the risk model)? A provider in the upper tail of real between-provider variation will flag here, correctly, because it does differ from the target.
    • Adjusted (pp, up and oep): is this provider unusual given the observed spread between providers? This treats the spread, τ, as "acceptable" variation [1, §5] and flags only providers outside it.

    funnel(compare_to=) names the two: "model" is the unadjusted chart of O/E against 1.0 (center=1.0, overdispersion=False), and "peers" the adjusted chart around the pooled value (the default settings). A fixed center with the adjustment on answers neither question (§4.2.1).

    Which question applies is a policy decision. Make it before seeing the data, and record it in the analysis plan [8, step 1]; Spiegelhalter calls the boundary between acceptable and out-of-control "a matter of judgment" [1, §5]. If the data are over-dispersed, the spread itself is a finding about the system: unmeasured case mix, data or coding differences, or real differences in practice. Spiegelhalter is explicit that adjusting is "purely a temporary measure" while its causes are explored [1, §5]. Every unadjusted funnel therefore reports the estimate (dispersion_phi, dispersion_tau, overdispersed) and, when it is present, a dispersion_note saying so.

    At individual-physician volumes the two usually coincide. τ is rarely detectable with 5–20 expected events per physician; on the bundled 24-surgeon data φ̂ = 0.93 and τ̂ = 0. When τ̂ = 0 the adjusted chart is the unadjusted chart, to the bit.

    4. Design choices¶

    4.1 Exact limits, interpolated¶

    Every funnel draws the exact discrete quantile with Spiegelhalter's continuity interpolation: r_p is the smallest count with F(r_p) ≥ p, and the limit is (r_p − α)/n with α = (F(r_p) − p)/f(r_p) [1, A.1.1]. The normal approximation of eq. 3 is symmetric and badly placed at the volumes individual providers have. At 5 expected events and O/E = 1, the 99.8% limits are:

    Method Lower Upper
    Exact Poisson (qikit) 0.00 2.55
    Normal, natural scale (eq. 3) 0 (clipped) 2.38
    Normal, square-root scale 0.10 2.86
    Normal, log scale 0.25 3.98

    The transformed-scale versions flag a zero-death physician (P = 0.7%) or a three-death physician against ten expected (P = 1.0%) as better than expected at the nominal 0.1%. The natural-scale version's upper limit is too tight, so it over-flags high outliers.

    R's qpois/qbinom return r_p without the interpolation, so an external cross-check should bracket qikit's value between r_p − 1 and r_p.

    The upper contour sits at the exact test's boundary. For an integer count O the interpolated quantile q = r_p − α decides the two sides differently:

    • the point is below the lower contour exactly when P(X ≤ O) < α, which is the exact test;
    • the point is above the upper contour q exactly when P(X > O) < α, which leaves out the probability of the observed count itself.

    So qikit draws the upper contour at q + 1 on the count scale (capped at n for a proportion). With that, a point is beyond either contour exactly when its conventional exact p-value, P(X ≥ O) above or P(X ≤ O) below (§4.11), is below the contour's tail probability. The drawn line, p_value, the point's color and, on the unadjusted O/E chart, "the point's Garwood 95% interval excludes the center line" are then one statement. The test suite checks all three on random funnels of every chart.

    The model, center line, τ² and levels are Spiegelhalter's; only the drawn upper boundary moves, by at most one count. At 5 expected events and θ₀ = 1 the 99.8% upper limit is 13.77 events (O/E 2.75) rather than the published 12.77 (2.55). Compared with other funnel software that draws the published contour, qikit is up to one count more conservative above the line at small volumes. This is a qikit derivation. As far as we know it is not a published contour, though the test it draws is the standard exact test, and also the comparison of a provider's exact interval with the benchmark.

    Shape at small counts. Spiegelhalter's upper contour wiggles at small denominators: on the ratio scale it climbs briefly within every integer step before falling again. Within a step α runs from 1 to 0 as λ = θ₀n grows, and q = r_p − α has slope q′(λ) = 1 + α(r_p/λ − 1), so q/λ has slope proportional to q′λ − q, which is 1 at the start of every step. The contour qikit draws, (q + 1)/λ, has slope proportional to

    q′λ − (q + 1) = (1 − α)(λ − r_p − 1),

    which is never positive, since an upper quantile r_p is at least λ. So it is monotone by construction, in all four families. On dense grids the published contour climbs back thousands of times (largest climb 0.067 on O/E for E between 0.3 and 2, 0.021 on a proportion at θ₀ = 0.1); the drawn contour never does, and no display smoothing is needed. The lower contours were already monotone.

    To start the axis where volumes become informative, use x_range=(2, None) on an O/E funnel or about n = 20 on a proportion, or spread the axis with x_log=True.

    4.2 Center line¶

    The default is the pooled rate (Σy/Σn, or ΣO/ΣE), which judges providers against their peers and, for O/E, absorbs overall miscalibration of the risk model. center=1.0 judges O/E against the risk model itself. The pooled line includes each provider. On a small cohort, one extreme provider drags the line toward itself, leaves every other provider to one side, and inflates τ̂ even though its own residual is winsorized. This is the circularity [1, §5] attributes to Marshall et al. The test suite pins a case where it hides the provider entirely (test_a_lone_outlier_still_leaks_in_through_the_pooled_center_line). Anchoring with center= removes the effect. Pavlou et al. [9] suggest a pre-specified τ as another remedy (§6).

    4.2.1 A fixed center under the adjustment: the absorbed gap¶

    τ̂ is estimated around whatever center line the chart uses. Around the pooled value it measures the spread between providers. Around a fixed center= it also absorbs any system-wide gap between the providers and that center: a whole system running 10% above its risk model looks, to the moment estimator, like providers spread widely around 1.0. Every limit then widens by that gap, and the chart answers neither question of §2. It is not judging against the model, because the allowance has swallowed the gap, and it is not judging against peers, because the line is not theirs.

    On 60 simulated physicians' stays (a system running 10% long, three physicians 40% worse on top, within_dispersion="estimate"), the default chart has τ̂ = 0.035 and flags the three. With center=1.0 alone τ̂ rises to 0.136, the limits at the median volume widen by about 112%, and one of the three falls back to an alert. With compare_to="model" (no allowance) 13 physicians are outliers and 23 alerts: the system-wide gap is now visible as signal, which is what that question asks.

    The guard. When center= is fixed, overdispersion=True and τ is estimated (not tau=), funnel() compares the providers' pooled value Σnᵢyᵢ/Σnᵢ with the center and warns when both of the following hold:

    1. The gap is beyond chance and the spread between providers: a two-sided p < 0.001 from a t test on K − 1 degrees of freedom with the robust (sandwich) variance K/(K − 1)·Σnᵢ²(yᵢ − ȳ)²/(Σnᵢ)², on the model's nᵢ (÷ φ). The observed scatter carries chance, within-case clumping and real spread together, so the test needs no τ̂. A first version used the model variance ȳ/Σnᵢ + τ̂²Σnᵢ²/(Σnᵢ)² instead. At K = 20 it warned in up to 3.6% of no-gap cohorts, because the published estimator understates τ at small K (§4.3).
    2. The gap matters: the 99.8% half-width at the median provider's volume, √(V₀ + τ̂²) around the fixed center against √(V₀ + τ̂ₚ²) with τ̂ₚ the same estimator around the pooled value, is at least 10% wider.

    The warning gives the gap and the widening, and names the fix: compare_to="model" on O/E, overdispersion=False with the target on a proportion or rate, or no center= for the peer question. summary reports center_gap (pooled ÷ center − 1), center_gap_p and tau_pooled. The calibration is in §5.

    4.3 The over-dispersion estimator: published by default, debiased on request¶

    overdispersion() in core/dispersion.py implements [1, §5]:

    1. Pearson residuals zᵢ = (yᵢ − θ₀)/√V₀ᵢ, with V₀ᵢ = θ₀/nᵢ for counts and θ₀(1 − θ₀)/nᵢ for proportions (eq. 4).
    2. Winsorize at the 10th and 90th percentiles; φ̂ = mean(z_W²) (eqs. 6, 8).
    3. τ̂² = (Kφ̂ − (K − 1)) / (Σwᵢ − Σwᵢ²/Σwᵢ), wᵢ = 1/V₀ᵢ (eq. 10, the DerSimonian–Laird form [3]), and τ̂² = 0 when φ̂ < (K − 1)/K.

    That is the default, tau_method="spiegelhalter", exactly as published. CQC and NHS SHMI run the same uncorrected method of moments on a transformed scale instead; "cqc" and "shmi" reproduce those, as FunnelPlotR [13] does (§4.17).

    The consistency factor. Winsorizing a standard normal at its p and 1 − p quantiles z = Φ⁻¹(1 − p) leaves E[Z_W²] = (1 − 2p) − 2z·φ(z) + 2p·z², which is 0.6787 at p = 0.10. Spiegelhalter derives the correction c(p) = 1/E[Z_W²] = 1.4735 (eq. 9), but reports that it gave "somewhat wide limits" and does not adopt it in his example. tau_method="winsorized" applies it: φ̂ = c(0.10)·mean(z_W²).

    Which to use, and why the published one is the default. Without the factor, φ̂ averages 0.68 under pure chance, so the τ² = 0 gate needs a true over-dispersion of about 1.47 before it moves. Small real spreads between providers are then ignored, the adjusted limits stay at their unadjusted width, and more providers flag. The table below (200 replicates of K = 1,000 providers, E ~ U(2, 60), Poisson counts with true relative spread τ) shows the size of the effect:

    true τ φ̂ published τ̂ published τ̂ debiased τ̂ plain DL (no winsorizing)
    0 0.678 0 0.017 0.016
    0.10 0.881 0 in every replicate 0.098 0.100
    0.20 1.452 0.120 0.192 0.200
    0.30 2.387 0.212 0.285 0.300
    0.10, plus 2% gross outliers (O/E 2.5) 0.942 0.004 0.113 0.230

    The two estimators answer the screening question differently:

    • The published estimator is the more sensitive screen. It flags more providers whose difference is the ordinary spread between peers (§5: 0.54% of in-distribution providers beyond the 99.8% line at K = 150, τ = 0.3, against a nominal 0.1%), and it finds a planted outlier more often (70% against 38% there). qikit 0.10 made the debiased estimator the default; in production use at physician scale it was not sensitive enough for screening, so 1.0 restores the published one. It is also uncorrected, as the CQC, NHS and FunnelPlotR estimators are, so its flag rates are comparable with theirs.
    • The debiased estimator holds the stated chance-flag rate. Use tau_method="winsorized" when flags are acted on individually and the 0.1% and 2.5% in expected_false_flags must mean what they say, as on a large funnel feeding credentialing. It stays robust to the outliers being screened for: plain DerSimonian–Laird doubles τ̂ when 2% of providers are gross outliers, the winsorized estimators do not.

    funnel_sensitivity() runs a funnel under each τ option and marks the providers whose tier depends on the choice; a flag that does is a judgment to make explicitly.

    A center line fixed in advance. Equation 10 counts K − 1 degrees of freedom because θ₀ is estimated from the same providers. With center= given (O/E against 1.0, say) no mean is estimated, and the method-of-moments estimate is τ̂² = K(φ̂ − 1)/Σwᵢ, 0 when φ̂ ≤ 1. "winsorized" uses that form; the published methods keep theirs, since reproducing a published funnel is their purpose. Note also that a fixed center absorbs any overall gap between the cohort and the benchmark into τ̂ (§4.2).

    Reproducing published funnels. The default gives the published estimator bit for bit; the shared Python/TypeScript fixtures that check Spiegelhalter's numbers pin it, and funnel_oep_winsorized, funnel_pp_winsorized and funnel_up_winsorized check "winsorized" in both engines. summary["phi"] is the dispersion ratio as the chosen method defines it (c·mean(z_W²) under "winsorized"). On the bundled example (data/examples/physician_mortality_oe.csv, three years pooled) τ̂ is 0 under the default and 0.113 debiased, and the same three physicians are outliers either way.

    Pearson rather than square-root residuals. qikit 0.4's oep used the square-root scale, 2(√O − √(θ₀E)), as the CQC variant does, on the grounds that Pearson residuals are skewed at small counts. After winsorizing, the difference is negligible at physician volumes: simulated τ̂ is within 0.015 of the square-root variant, and the bundled surgeons give τ̂ = 0 either way. The natural scale is [1]'s, and it feeds eq. 11 directly, with no delta-method mapping.

    4.4 Additive, not multiplicative¶

    Spiegelhalter presents both models and illustrates the difference in his Figure 6. Multiplicative φ inflates every provider's variance by the same factor, so the band keeps closing onto θ₀ at high volume. Additive τ² adds a constant between-provider variance, so the band is "essentially independent of volume" there [1, §5]. His companion paper finds the additive formulation has the stronger conceptual foundation [2], and it is what CQC, NHS SHMI and FunnelPlotR use. Pavlou et al. [9] reach the same preference for random-effects approaches when the common-mean assumption fails.

    For physician comparisons the choice matters at both ends of the funnel:

    • Small volume. V₀ dominates, and additive τ² barely moves the limits. Multiplicative φ widens them most, in absolute terms, exactly where power is already 5–44%.
    • Large volume. Additive limits level off at roughly θ₀ ± z·τ, so a busy provider is not flagged for a difference smaller than the spread between providers.

    qikit 0.4's multiplicative oep had a further defect. When φ̂ was clamped to 1 it still drew normal-approximation limits, 7–10% tighter than oe at E ≤ 8. Switching to oep "to be safe" therefore produced more high flags. In 0.5, oep with τ̂ = 0 is oe.

    4.5 Adjusted limits drawn exactly¶

    Eq. 11 describes a model in which providers' true rates θᵢ are spread around θ₀ with variance τ², and observed counts are Poisson or binomial given θᵢ. Drawing θᵢ from a gamma distribution (counts) or a beta distribution (proportions) with that mean and variance gives the classic hierarchical model for standardized ratios [7], whose marginal distributions are exact:

    Family Count distribution Variance on the indicator scale
    Counts (up and oep) Poisson–gamma (negative binomial), size θ₀²/τ² θ₀/n + τ² (eq. 11 exactly)
    Proportions (pp) Beta-binomial, α + β = θ₀(1 − θ₀)/τ² − 1 θ₀(1 − θ₀)/n + τ²(n − 1)/n

    qikit draws the interpolated quantiles of these, as §4.1 does for the unadjusted ones. This is the one methodological departure from [1]: same model and same variance, exact rather than normal quantiles. It is the substitution [1, Appendix A] itself makes for the unadjusted limits. Neither Verburg et al. [8] nor Pavlou et al. [9] evaluate it. Verburg uses multiplicative φ̂ because the additive model could not be combined with exact binomial limits. Pavlou uses the additive model on the log scale and accepts the normal approximation, warning that it may fail at small counts. The mixture distributions remove that trade-off.

    Implementation notes (dist.py, stats.ts):

    • Arithmetic only. Each pmf is built from its term ratio f(k+1)/f(k) outward from the mode and normalized at the end. It uses only + − × ÷, so results are bit-identical across platforms and between Python and TypeScript. There is no lgamma cancellation as τ² → 0, and the cost scales with the distribution's spread, not its mean.
    • Validated against scipy.stats (binom, nbinom, betabinom) to < 1e-9, and one beta-binomial case against 60-digit mpmath. There scipy itself is off by ~1e-5, from the cancellation this design avoids.
    • Fallbacks. A spread no beta distribution can have (τ² ≥ θ₀(1 − θ₀)), or a distribution too wide to walk (only absurd τ), falls back to eq. 11 as published, with a proportion's contours kept between 0 and 1. summary["limit_method"] then says "normal" instead of "exact". A non-integer denominator is interpolated between its whole neighbors (§4.13), and limit_method says "interpolated".
    • Computation. From 0.10 the walk is vectorized in blocks after its first 64 terms, with the upward and downward running products done by np.multiply.accumulate and np.divide.accumulate and every sum by np.cumsum. All three are strictly sequential, so every term, the point where the walk stops, every tail sum, and therefore every limit and p-value are bit-identical to the scalar loop the TypeScript twin keeps (checked on 36,000 random calls across all four families). A walk, and each Poisson mean inversion, is cached per distribution, because a point's two contours and its p-value walk the same pmf. A 1,000-provider oep funnel at hospital volumes (E from 30 to 2,000) takes about 0.3 s cold, down from 1.3 s, and 0.06 s when the same distributions recur. scipy is not used. It is not a dependency, its incomplete-gamma and beta routines differ from this walk in the last ulps across platforms, and its beta-binomial loses about 1e-5 to the cancellation described above. The cold time is dominated by the lgamma-based Poisson mean inversion behind intervals and detectability. Solving all providers' inversions at once would make it faster, but it moves the last quantized digit of some results at counts in the thousands, so it has not been done.

    4.6 Laney's p′/u′ stay for time order¶

    Laney's σ_z is the mean moving range of consecutive standardized residuals [6]. That statistic depends on order and is defined for time series [11]. In a funnel the order is the sort by volume. On the bundled 3-year surgeon data, the published test finds no over-dispersion, yet the 0.4 up funnel widened its limits 1.87× and hid all three real signals. Since 0.5 a funnel uses the additive method, and Laney's pp/up remain only as time-ordered charts in qic().

    4.7 Warn tier and multiplicity¶

    The 99.8% contour marks outliers and the 95% contour alerts (the signal column: "outlier", "alert" or "none"). The tiers never overlap, and alerts are counted in n_alert, apart from n_outlier, because at one chance alert in forty per side a large funnel would always report one. About 0.1% of average providers land beyond each 99.8% line, roughly one in a 1,000-provider one-sided funnel, and 2.5% beyond each 95% line, about 24 there. summary["expected_false_flags"] reports those numbers for the chart, so an alert count is read against them. [1, §6] recommends formal FDR control [12] carried out separately from the plot. Every funnel reports Benjamini–Hochberg q-values, and signal="fdr" flags by them (§4.11).

    4.8 Power and intervals¶

    Not part of [1]. At physician volumes a funnel mostly cannot detect even a doubling of risk [10], and a blank funnel reads as a clean bill of health. This is in the spirit of Verburg's sample-size step [8, step 3], with a doubling rather than 1.5× as the reference effect. Power is fixed at 80% and is not a parameter: on a chart that feeds credentialing, a tunable threshold invites reporting whichever number reads best.

    The detectable effect. It is measured against the limit the chart actually draws, including the wider limits of oep/pp/up: the question is whether this point would flag on this chart. Signals fire strictly above ucl, so a count flags when X ≥ ⌊ucl·n⌋ + 1. The smallest true effect with 80% power is the one at which F(⌊ucl·n⌋) = 0.2. With the upper contour at the exact test's boundary (§4.1) that event is exactly "p < 0.001". The equation is solved exactly in the CDF's own parameter, never by scanning over ratios. funnel() reports the result as min_detectable and the interval as ci_lower/ci_upper on every measure:

    Engine chart Count model Solved for Engine column (y scale) Per-point 95% CI
    oe, oep Poisson(ρE) mean, then ρ = μ/E min_detectable_oe Garwood [5]
    u, up funnel Poisson(θn) mean, then θ = μ/n min_detectable Garwood [5]
    p, pp funnel Binomial(n, θ) θ directly min_detectable Clopper–Pearson [14]

    A u funnel's algebra is the O/E chart's with exposure in place of expected events, so it reuses the same functions. A Clopper–Pearson interval inverts the exact binomial test the same way Garwood inverts the Poisson one: the lower bound is the θ at which x or more events has probability 0.025, and the upper bound is the θ at which x or fewer does.

    The Poisson solve takes safeguarded Newton steps on log F, where F = f·S, S is a running sum of products of j/μ, and d log F/dμ = −1/S. Six to ten steps replace fifty full CDF sweeps. A thousand-provider funnel with counts in the thousands (length of stay in days) took about 17 s by bisection and now takes well under 1 s. The TypeScript engine still bisects; the two agree far inside the quantization below.

    Cross-platform reproducibility. The Poisson solve uses one lgamma, whose last ulp differs between platforms, so it is quantized to 1e-9. The binomial solve is lgamma-free. Its CDF is built from the term ratio outward from the mode, using the same walk as the binomial limits (§4.5), and normalized by its own total, so (1 − θ)ⁿ, which underflows at hospital volumes, is never formed. The walk uses only + − × ÷. The θ solve takes safeguarded Newton steps on the log of the smaller tail from a Wilson-score start, about five CDF evaluations instead of fifty, and stops once a step is below 1e-13 of θ. A proportion funnel of 5,000 providers fell from 10 s to about 1 s. The TypeScript engine still bisects; the two agree to about 1e-13 relative. Contours, which must sit exactly on the test, keep their own bisection and are bit-identical across engines.

    The summary. Every chart reports underpowered, n_underpowered and, when underpowered, a quotable power_note. Ghosted (exclude=) points are left out: they cannot flag, so they say nothing about the chart's reach.

    • O/E. The test is min_detectable_oe > 2, with the median reported as min_detectable_oe_median.
    • p/u funnels. A proportion or rate has no natural unit, so the test is relative to the center line: min_detectable / cl > 2, with the median of that ratio reported as min_detectable_ratio_median. On a p funnel with cl ≥ 0.5 a doubling is not a proportion. There only points that cannot flag at all count as underpowered; the ratio still says how far above the line a point would have to be.

    Edge cases. A point whose limit reaches n cannot flag at any θ. Internally its detectable proportion is infinite, so it counts as underpowered and enters the median; the column shows NaN. With exact interpolated limits this is effectively unreachable. A non-integer denominator (n/φ under within_dispersion, or an averaged n) has no binomial description. Its detectability and interval are interpolated between the whole neighbors, as its limits are (§4.13).

    4.9 The volume axis¶

    [1, §2] plots each indicator against its precision ρ = 1/s², with the limits drawn as smooth contours. qikit plots against the denominator instead: sample size on p/pp, exposure on u/up, and expected events on oe/oep. That is the same axis up to a constant. For a proportion s² = θ₀(1 − θ₀)/n, so ρ ∝ n. For a rate or an O/E ratio s² = θ₀/n (or θ₀/E), so ρ ∝ n (or E). The difference is that volume is a unit a reader already understands. The adjusted funnels use the same axis, which is where the levelling-off of §4.4 shows.

    This is the axis funnel() draws. A funnel whose denominators are all equal falls back to plotting by unit, because a volume axis would stack every point on one line. The y axis is fitted to the providers, the center line and each contour's value nearest the center line, so the low-volume flare does not set the scale.

    The contours. FunnelResult.limits() evaluates the limits on about 200 geometric steps between the smallest and largest denominator, merged with every point's own n. It calls the same limit functions on the same path as the per-point limits (core.contours.funnel_contours), with the unrounded τ², so each curve passes exactly through the limit that decided that point's signal. A drawn point can therefore never sit inside the curve while being flagged, or outside it while not being flagged. A test pins this bit-for-bit on every funnel fixture. Between grid steps the plot interpolates linearly.

    Binomial charts step in whole numbers. On p/pp the grid is rounded to integers, where the limits are exact. A non-integer n is interpolated between its whole neighbors (§4.13), so the contours stay continuous through every step, including under within_dispersion, where the model sees n/φ. The Poisson and Poisson–gamma charts take any positive n.

    Log scale. x_log=True log-scales the axis, which spreads out a funnel whose providers span orders of magnitude. Plotly takes an annotation's position on a log axis as log10 of the value, so the center-line and limit labels and the signal labels are placed on that scale.

    4.10 One-sided signalling¶

    Many indicators matter in one direction only; excess mortality is the usual example. funnel(worse="higher") flags only points beyond the upper contours, and worse="lower" only those beyond the lower ones. worse=None, the default, is the two-sided chart of [1]. The filter applies to both tiers, and through them to the point colors, the legend ("Worse than expected: …"), the counts and label_signals=. summary["worse"] records it.

    • Each side keeps its own tail probability. A one-sided chart flags at 0.1% and 2.5% per provider by chance, half the two-sided chart's 0.2% and 5%.
    • The other side's limits are still drawn, because they are still where the null model puts them. Hide them with styles={"lcl": {"visible": False}, "lcl_95": {"visible": False}}.
    • Only on funnels. Runs rules describe patterns, not points beyond a line, and are never side-filtered, so qic() has no side argument.
    • Detectability is still about the upper limit. min_detectable describes the upper limit whatever the side. Under worse="lower" it says what an above-center provider would have needed, which the chart no longer flags.

    4.11 P-values, the false discovery rate, and what decides a flag¶

    Every funnel reports two values per point:

    • p_value: an exact p-value against the null model its limits are drawn from;
    • q_value: its Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment [15], the procedure [12] applies to provider comparisons.

    The null model. It uses the same distribution, center line and τ² as the limits. summary["count_model"] names it:

    Engine chart count_model Count X
    oe, u poisson Poisson(θ₀n)
    oep, up poisson_gamma (poisson when τ̂ = 0) Poisson–gamma, mean θ₀n, size θ₀²/τ² [7]
    p binomial Binomial(n, θ₀)
    pp beta_binomial (binomial when τ̂ = 0) Binomial(n, θ), θ ~ Beta(θ₀s, (1 − θ₀)s), s = θ₀(1 − θ₀)/τ² − 1

    A few rules follow from using the limits' own model:

    • The unadjusted charts ignore their diagnostic τ̂, as their limits do.
    • θ₀ is treated as known, as in [1].
    • Under within_dispersion=φ the model sees O/φ events against E/φ (§4.12).

    The p-value. These are conventional exact tail probabilities of the observed count O = y·n:

    worse p-value
    "higher" P(X ≥ O)
    "lower" P(X ≤ O)
    None min(1, 2·min of the two)

    They are valid p-values (uniform or conservative under the null), which the Benjamini–Hochberg step needs.

    • Computation. Each tail is summed directly from its own terms, never as one minus the other, on the lgamma-free walk the mixture quantiles use (§4.5). A small p-value therefore keeps its digits, and the result is identical across platforms. Terms the walk drops sit below 1e-16 of the mass, so p-values smaller than that read 0.
    • A count between whole numbers. This is an averaged aggregate, or an effective count O/φ. Each tail is interpolated linearly between its two integer neighbors. That is exactly the inverse of the interpolated quantile, so the line-equals-test property below holds for these counts too. summary["p_value_method"] is then "interpolated".
    • A denominator between whole numbers (binomial only: n/φ under within_dispersion). Each tail is interpolated in n as well, by the function the contours are solved from (§4.13). p_value_method is "interpolated".
    • Normal fallback. Where the limits fall back to the normal approximation, the p-values do too, with the same mean and variance and no continuity correction:
    • a pp spread no beta distribution can have;
    • a walk past its term limit.

    p_value_method is "normal" if any point fell back.

    The line is the test. With the upper contour drawn at the exact test's boundary (§4.1), a point is beyond a contour exactly when its p-value is below that contour's tail probability. The tail probability is 0.001 and 0.025 one-sided at the default 99.8% and 95%, doubled when two-sided. The test suite checks this on random funnels of all six charts, for whole and fractional counts, at both levels and on both sides. With 13 deaths against 5 expected, for example, P(X ≥ 13) = 0.20%: the point is an alert, between the 95% and 99.8% lines. Under the published contour it sat beyond the 99.8% line.

    signal picks one test for both tiers:

    signal outlier alert
    "limits" (default) beyond the outer contour, i.e. p < its tail probability beyond the inner contour, not an outlier
    "fdr" q_value < fdr_levels[0] (0.05) q_value < fdr_levels[1] (0.10), not an outlier

    summary["expected_false_flags"] gives each tier's chance count. Under "limits" it is K·α: the providers a chart of K average providers would put in the tier. Under "fdr" it is the Benjamini–Hochberg bound, at most Q times the number flagged.

    Why there is no union of the two. A downstream request asked for "beyond the limit OR q below its level", either test being enough, as the default. The q-value at rank i is below Q exactly when p ≤ Q·R/K, where R is the number of Benjamini–Hochberg discoveries. So the union is the same as p < max(α, Q·R/K):

    • At the alert tier (α = 0.025, Q = 0.10), q adds a flag only when more than a quarter of providers are flagged, which is essentially never. The ~25 chance alerts in 1,000 that motivate FDR all stay.
    • At the outlier tier (α = 0.001, Q = 0.05), q adds flags only when more than 2% of providers are discoveries. That is, it adds power where signals are already plentiful, which is the opposite of the stated aim.

    The union's tier also carries neither guarantee: it is not "about α of average providers", nor "at most Q of these are false". Each pure mode gives a clinical reader one sentence. A "flagged on at least two measures" ranking rule is itself a strong multiplicity guard: with four measures and independent chance alerts, about 6 × 0.025² ≈ 0.4% of average providers qualify.

    The q-value. Take the K points that have a p-value (summary["n_tested"]; excluded points have none) and sort them by p-value, ascending. The q-value at rank i is the minimum over ranks j ≥ i of p₍ⱼ₎·K/j, capped at 1. A point is significant at false discovery rate Q exactly when q < Q. Each chart, and each facet, is adjusted on its own. x_range= only trims the view and never changes K.

    On the figure.

    • Caption. A figure without a caption of its own states the rule that colored its points: the exact test and its levels, or the FDR levels and that the curves are then for reference only.
    • Coloring by q on a "limits" chart. The two tests disagree most at the inner contour: a point just beyond the 95% line passes that line's exact test, but among hundreds of providers its q can be 0.3. .plot(color_by="fdr") colors the points by the "fdr" tiers at fdr_levels while the result, its signal column and the drawn contours stay "limits", so a figure can match an action list built from q_value. fdr_watch= adds a looser, display-only tier (open diamonds). The caption then states the q rule.
    • Hover. It shows each point's p and q.
    • Detectability. min_detectable describes the drawn outer contour, which under "limits" is the test itself. Under "fdr" what a point needs depends on every other point.

    Filtering before the FDR step. fdr_min_denominator=t leaves out of the Benjamini–Hochberg step every provider whose denominator, as the model sees it (n/φ under within_dispersion), is below t. Those providers keep their p_value but get no q_value, so they cannot flag under signal="fdr", and they do not count in its K. Nothing else moves: they still count toward θ₀ and τ², are still drawn, and still flag under signal="limits". summary["n_filtered"] counts them.

    • Why this is valid. A filter that depends only on each test's design (its denominator) and not on its outcome is independent filtering [29]. Under the null the kept p-values are still uniform or conservative, so Benjamini–Hochberg on the kept set still controls the false discovery rate, now over fewer tests.
    • Underpowered is not untestable. For worse="higher" on counts the upper tail is unbounded. A physician with E = 1.5 and 8 readmissions has a tiny p-value, so low volume never makes a provider unable to flag, only unlikely to. A floor on volume is therefore a policy choice to give up those rare gross low-volume outliers in exchange for power among the rest. That is why it is opt-in and named for what it does. A truly untestable provider, whose smallest attainable p-value is above the threshold, exists only on a bounded tail: worse="lower" on counts (p ≥ e^(−θ₀E) at zero events), and proportions at very small n. That is Tarone's criterion [30], and the discrete FDR procedures built on it [31, 32] are the principled way to gain power there (planned, §6).
    • Declined: Storey's q-value [33] and a power-screened BH. Storey's estimate of the null share π₀ gains power only when many providers are truly non-null. With τ² in the null model, most providers are null by construction, so π̂₀ ≈ 1 and the gain is small. Discrete, conservative p-values pile up near 1 and push π̂₀ toward 1 further. A screen on min_detectable that sets q_value to NaN would remove the gross low-volume outliers above, so it was declined as a default and replaced by this explicit floor.

    4.12 Counts that clump within cases¶

    Spiegelhalter's count models assume each counted unit is an independent event. Length of stay as observed/expected days breaks that: one stay adds many days at once, so a provider's day total has variance about φ times its mean, where φ is the variance-to-mean ratio of days per stay. φ is typically 5–10 for inpatient stays. The additive τ² cannot absorb this. It adds a constant to the variance of the ratio, while the missing variance, φθ₀/E, shrinks with volume, so the smallest providers' limits stay far too narrow and every p- and q-value is too small (§5).

    The fix. within_dispersion=φ treats the count as a Poisson count of O/φ events against E/φ expected: the quasi-Poisson model, in which variance is φ times the mean. The ratio is unchanged, so only the denominator the model sees moves. The limits, τ² (Pearson z with variance θ₀φ/E), p-values (interpolated for the non-integer effective counts, §4.11), detectability and intervals all follow from it. The echoed numerator and denominator stay in the caller's units. Effective counts are large at length-of-stay volumes.

    φ below 1. A binary-outcome O/E (deaths, readmissions) from case rows has variance Σp(1 − p) < Σp = E, so "estimate" returns φ̂ ≈ 1 − p̄ there. qikit keeps the estimate, which narrows the limits by the binomial variance the Poisson model leaves out (about 8% at a 15% event rate, negligible for mortality), and adds a note saying so; within_dispersion=1 keeps the Poisson model. The effective counts are then fractional, so the test is interpolated (§4.11).

    Estimating φ. within_dispersion="estimate" takes stay-level rows (several per unit) and computes

    φ̂ = Σⱼ Σᵢ (yᵢⱼ − θ̂ⱼnᵢⱼ)² / (θ̂ⱼnᵢⱼ) / (N − K), θ̂ⱼ = Σᵢyᵢⱼ / Σᵢnᵢⱼ,

    the Pearson dispersion of each stay around its own provider's rate. Using each provider's own rate keeps real differences between providers out of φ̂, leaving them to τ²; the test suite checks that a planted provider effect leaves φ̂ unchanged. A faceted call estimates φ per facet. Long stays dominate φ̂, so cap them at a stated percentile before aggregating, as length-of-stay benchmarks usually do.

    The diagnostic. An agent or analyst who passes aggregated days without φ should not get a silently wrong chart. The Pearson residuals of any funnel have variance a + b·nᵢ/n̄, in two separable parts:

    • clumping within cases inflates every provider's variance by the same factor, whatever its volume, so a = φ;
    • real differences between providers add τ², which grows with volume on the z scale, so b > 0.

    qikit fits both by maximum likelihood, by iteratively reweighted least squares on z² with each residual capped at three of its own SDs. It calls the shape "within" when an intercept above 1 is significant (likelihood ratio > χ²₁ 0.999) and estimates φ ≥ 2, and "between" when a slope above 0 is significant at the same level. The result is summary["dispersion_shape"] (none / between / within / both, or unknown when the largest volume is under 1.5 times the smallest). It needs at least 30 providers, the smallest K the thresholds were calibrated at. When the shape is "within" or "both" and within_dispersion was left at 1, qikit:

    • raises a UserWarning naming the fix;
    • adds the same sentence to summary["notes"];
    • adds a warning line to the figure's caption.

    The intercept is reported as within_dispersion_hint, an estimate of φ from aggregated data alone. Because the fix is a model choice, the diagnostic never applies it.

    Estimating φ from SQL totals. Stay-level rows need not leave the database. With θ̂ⱼ = Yⱼ/Nⱼ, each provider's Pearson sum expands to sufficient statistics:

    • counts: Σᵢ (yᵢⱼ − θ̂ⱼnᵢⱼ)²/(θ̂ⱼnᵢⱼ) = Σᵢ yᵢⱼ²/nᵢⱼ / θ̂ⱼ − Yⱼ;
    • proportions: Σᵢ (yᵢⱼ − θ̂ⱼnᵢⱼ)²/nᵢⱼ = Σᵢ yᵢⱼ²/nᵢⱼ − θ̂ⱼYⱼ.

    So one row per provider of (Y, N, k = cases, Σy²/n), passed as cases= and sum_sq=, gives φ̂, the clustering check, and every downstream number equal to the case-row path to rounding (the test suite checks both families). sum_sq is a raw sum, not a residual, so SQL cannot compute it around the wrong center.

    One φ per provider: case mix. A single φ assumes every provider's stays are equally variable. When variability depends on what is treated (DRG, service line), a provider with complex patients has a larger true φ. within_dispersion="<column>" takes one φᵢ per provider. The natural construction is case-mix weighting: φ_g per DRG from SQL, then φᵢ = Σⱼ φ_g(j)·eⱼ / Σⱼ eⱼ over the provider's stays. Each provider is judged on Yᵢ/φᵢ out of Nᵢ/φᵢ, the same quasi-Poisson effective counts as above. Because no single φ maps caller units to the model, the x axis (and limits()) is the effective denominator (effective_denominator), so each point still sits against its own exact test. numerator and denominator stay in the caller's units, and summary["within_dispersion"] is the volume-weighted mean Σn/Σ(n/φᵢ). A φ_g estimated from few stays is noisy. Shrinking it toward the pooled φ with the moderated-variance step of Smyth [19] (prior degrees of freedom estimated, not fixed) is the right use of that idea; a built-in strata= that does this is planned (§6).

    Declined: each provider's own variance. A request asked for an O/E chart on which each provider's null variance comes from its own stays, s²ᵢ shrunk toward the pool (limma's moderated t [19]), tested on t with ν₀ + k − 1 degrees of freedom. That transposes a genomics method to a setting where it fails:

    • Self-masking. Length of stay is right-skewed, and its variance rises with its mean, so a provider with long stays also has a large s²ᵢ. Their own z shrinks, and the providers the funnel exists to find hide behind their own variability. Meta-analysis has the same problem. Standard methods treat estimated within-study variances as known and ignore their correlation with the effect estimates, which Jackson and White call potentially serious in small studies [20, §3.2].
    • The center leaks into the variance. Residuals taken around θ₀ rather than the provider's own rate give E[s²ᵢ] = Var + (θᵢ − θ₀)²·(spread of e), so the deviation being tested also inflates its own variance.
    • The line stops being the test. A funnel's null variance must describe an average provider treating this provider's patients [1, 21]: a function of the patients, not of this provider's outcomes. Contours drawn from a pooled s₀² while each point is tested against its own s²ᵢ break the invariant that a point beyond a line is flagged (§4.11).
    • An arbitrary prior. ν₀ = 20 fixed, where Smyth estimates it, on top of a second normal approximation.

    The two needs behind the request, variability that differs by case mix and no 200,000-row transfer, are met by the column and the sufficient statistics above, without these failures.

    4.13 Multi-item instruments: items clustered in surveys¶

    A survey composite scored per item, such as HCAHPS "Communication with Doctors" ("Always" answers / items answered over explains, listens and respects), is a proportion of items. The items on one survey come from one patient and mostly agree. A binomial treats them as independent. If the m items of a survey have intraclass correlation ρ, a provider's item count has variance

    n θ(1 − θ) · [1 + (m − 1)ρ] = n θ(1 − θ) · φ,

    where φ is Kish's design effect [18]. With three items and ρ = 0.7, φ ≈ 2.4. Twelve items from four surveys then carry about as much information as five independent ones.

    Why item counts over-flag, and why the flags move. Left at φ = 1, the funnel makes two errors:

    • Small providers are over-flagged. Every provider's binomial limits are √φ too narrow. The additive τ² cannot correct this, because the missing variance shrinks with volume (§4.12). Every p-value and q-value is too small as well.
    • τ̂ is inflated. τ̂ absorbs part of the clustering variance as if providers really differed, which widens the limits of high-volume providers.

    Correcting φ therefore does more than remove flags. τ̂ falls, small providers drop out, and some large providers become more clearly unusual.

    Which unit to pass. Pass one row per survey: y = items meeting the measure, n = items answered. Use measure="proportion" and within_dispersion="estimate". Two other inputs fail:

    • One row per item. This hides the problem. A row holding a single yes/no has variance fixed by its mean, so it cannot show clustering, and "estimate" refuses rows whose n are all 1.
    • One row per provider. This leaves only the provider-level shape test (§4.12), which a real between-provider spread can mask. In a downstream example with φ = 2.4 on both top-box and bottom-box, the totals read "within" on top-box but "between" on bottom-box.

    Recoding the measure as "all items Always", one binary per survey, would be an exact binomial. It is a different measure, though, not the published HCAHPS definition.

    The fix. within_dispersion=φ gives the model y/φ events out of n/φ cases, the Rao–Scott effective sample size for clustered binary data [17]. It is the binomial analogue of the quasi-Poisson correction in §4.12. The proportion is unchanged.

    Estimating φ. From survey-level rows:

    φ̂ = Σⱼ Σᵢ (yᵢⱼ − θ̂ⱼnᵢⱼ)² / nᵢⱼ / [θ̄(1 − θ̄)(N − K)],

    • Residuals are taken around each provider's own θ̂ⱼ, so differences between providers stay out of φ̂ (§4.12).
    • The variance uses the pooled θ̄, not each provider's θ̂ⱼ(1 − θ̂ⱼ). At a few surveys per provider many have θ̂ⱼ of 0 or 1. On a simulated bottom-box at 10% (§5), nearly one physician in five had no bottom-box answer at all. Their own rate would drop them, biasing φ̂.
    • With the pooled rate, independent yes/no rows read φ̂ ≈ 1, where their own rate would read N/(N − K).
    • The remaining bias is −τ²/[θ̄(1 − θ̄)]: under 1% at realistic τ.

    A denominator between whole numbers. n/φ is rarely an integer, and no binomial has a fractional size. Rounding it, the usual workaround, moves a five-item-equivalent provider by up to 10%. The normal approximation is badly placed at these counts (§4.1). qikit interpolates every tail linearly in ν = n/φ between m₀ = ⌊ν⌋ and m₀ + 1, at the same proportion p:

    T(p; ν) = (1 − w)·T_m₀(p·m₀) + w·T_m₀₊₁(p·(m₀ + 1)), w = ν − m₀.

    Each T_m is the exact binomial or beta-binomial tail, itself interpolated between whole counts (§4.11). Everything follows from this one function:

    • Contours. The contours are where T crosses each tail probability, found by bisection, so "beyond the line" and "p < α" remain one statement.
    • Whole ν. At a whole ν, T is the exact tail. The contours are then the exact ones, continuous through every integer and monotone in volume (both checked by the test suite).
    • Consistency across models. The same holds for the binomial and the beta-binomial, so τ̂ = 0 still reproduces the unadjusted chart to the bit.
    • Intervals and detectability. The 95% interval inverts the same tails in θ. That is Korn and Graubard's effective-sample-size Clopper–Pearson interval for survey data [16], with this interpolation in place of their beta form. min_detectable interpolates the chance of crossing the drawn line in the same way.
    • Labels. summary["limit_method"] and summary["p_value_method"] read "interpolated". The arithmetic is lgamma-free, as in §4.5.

    The case-level check. Case-level rows are several rows per unit: stays, or surveys. When they are present and within_dispersion is left at 1, qikit computes the row φ̂ above, or the count version of §4.12 for oe/rate, and tests it with Pearson χ² on N − K degrees of freedom. It warns when φ̂ ≥ 1.25 and p < 0.001. The floor is low because the test is powerful: at φ = 1.25 the 99.8% limits are √1.25 too narrow, which in the normal approximation lets through nearly three times the nominal false outliers. When it fires:

    • dispersion_shape becomes "within", or "both" if the totals also show "between";
    • within_dispersion_hint is the row φ̂, and within_dispersion_hint_source is "rows" (otherwise "totals");
    • the warning, note and caption line of §4.12 follow, naming one row per survey as the fix for a proportion.

    The check never applies the fix itself. Rows of one yes/no each, and rows that leave fewer than 30 residual degrees of freedom, are not tested.

    Reading physician-level composites. Four surveys at φ = 2.42 give about five effective items. Against a top-box rate of 80%, the 99.8% lower contour is then 1.8%: only a physician with almost no "Always" answers is an outlier. Against a bottom-box rate of 10%, the upper contour is 79%. min_detectable and underpowered say so per provider. For choosing physicians for intervention, prefer signal="fdr" with a one-sided worse=. Top-box and bottom-box are two tests of the same items, so a physician flagged on both has one finding, not two.

    4.14 Shrunk estimates and reliability¶

    Every funnel reports, per provider:

    Column Definition
    raw_excess (Y − θ₀N), signed so positive is worse: the observed excess days or events
    reliability B, the shrinkage weight below
    eb_rate B·y + (1 − B)·θ₀ (on the plotted scale)
    eb_excess (eb_rate − reference)·N, signed as raw_excess
    eb_low, eb_high Equal-tailed interval= (95%) quantiles of the provider's posterior
    prob_worse P(true value worse than the reference), from the same posterior
    estimate_flag Where [eb_low, eb_high] lies against the reference

    θ₀ here is the prior's center: the center line, or under compare_to="model" the pooled value (below). The reference is the center line, or 1.0 under compare_to="model" (summary["reference"]).

    The estimator is the chart's own posterior mean. The adjusted funnels already model true provider rates as gamma (counts) or beta (proportions) around θ₀ with variance τ² (§4.5). For those conjugate families the posterior mean of a provider's true rate is exactly linear in its observed rate: Bühlmann credibility [22]. For the Poisson–gamma model with a = θ₀²/τ², b = θ₀/τ², the posterior mean is (a + O)/(b + E), so B = τ²/(τ² + θ₀/E). For the beta-binomial with α + β = s = θ₀(1 − θ₀)/τ² − 1, it is (α + Y)/(s + n), so B = n/(n + s). Both use the effective n/φ under within_dispersion. There is no choice between a "linear" and an "exact" estimator to make: they are the same number. B is also the reliability of the provider's score in the sense of the physician-profiling literature, τ²/(τ² + V₀) [24]; for the beta-binomial it carries the model's (n − 1)/n factor.

    The prior's spread is estimated by maximum likelihood. The limits keep the chart's tau_method, but the estimate columns use τ̂ by maximum likelihood of the same mixture model around θ₀ (summary["estimate_tau"]), or tau= when given. The published estimator understates τ, often returning 0 for a real spread (§4.16). For a screen whose flags are reviewed, that errs toward flagging, which is acceptable. For an estimate, it over-shrinks every provider toward θ₀ and leaves the intervals short: with τ estimated by the published method, 95% intervals covered 49–89% of true values; by maximum likelihood, 91–95% (§5). Maximum likelihood is not robust to outliers (§4.16): a few extreme providers raise τ̂, which shrinks everyone less. For an estimate that is the cautious direction. Before 1.2 the estimate columns used the chart's τ, so their values changed with 1.2.

    Intervals. With a = θ₀²/τ², b = θ₀/τ², a provider's true value has posterior Gamma(a + O, rate b + E) on counts (O/E, rates, means), and with α = θ₀s, β = (1 − θ₀)s, Beta(α + Y, β + n − Y) on proportions, all on effective counts (O/φ out of E/φ). eb_low and eb_high are its (1 − c)/2 and (1 + c)/2 quantiles for interval= c, found by inverting the regularized incomplete gamma and beta functions with safeguarded Newton steps on log x (gamma) or logit x (beta); no dependency beyond numpy. estimate_flag is "worse" or "better" (with worse=; "higher" or "lower" without) when the whole interval lies on one side of the reference, and "no different" otherwise. It is the CMS reporting rule [23, 45] in this model, and it has no multiplicity control.

    Against the risk model. compare_to="model" draws limits around 1.0 with no spread, so the chart itself has no prior to shrink toward. The estimate columns then use a prior centered on the providers' pooled value Σnᵢyᵢ/Σnᵢ, with τ̂ estimated around that value (or tau=, which here sets only this spread). Each provider is judged against 1.0: prob_worse is P(true O/E > 1.0), and estimate_flag compares the interval with 1.0. The pooled center is deliberate. τ estimated around 1.0 would absorb any system-wide gap between the providers and the model (§4.2.1), and the pooled value is the best prior guess for a low-volume provider in this system. Limits, p-values, q-values and signal are exactly those of center=1.0, overdispersion=False. The same prior applies to that explicit pair.

    This adapts the CMS hospital-level method [45] to physicians, with an external expected (Vizient, CMS) as the baseline. It applies established methods [21, 23, 35, 46, 49]; it is not a replication of a published physician-level design. CMS fits a hierarchical logistic model on patient-level data with full uncertainty. funnel() uses the Poisson–gamma or beta-binomial model on provider totals, with τ plugged in. Its intervals are therefore somewhat narrower when τ is poorly determined (few providers). The example notebook (docs/examples/hierarchical_readmissions.ipynb) fits the patient-level model for comparison.

    No spread, no shrinkage target. When τ̂ = 0, every B is 0, every eb_rate is θ₀, every eb_excess is 0 against the center line, and the interval columns are NaN with a note. That is the honest answer: the data show no between-provider spread, so no provider's difference can be told from chance. No fallback weight is invented. An estimated τ that would leave even the largest provider's reliability below 0.01 counts as no spread: maximum likelihood returns such values from numerical noise beside the τ = 0 boundary, and their priors carry nothing. An unadjusted chart with a custom center (overdispersion=False, not against the risk model) has no prior either.

    Why rank on eb_excess. raw_excess scales with volume, so a high-volume provider with a small, noisy difference outranks a mid-volume provider with a large, certain one. eb_excess is the expected excess once chance is discounted. It is a defensible prioritization loss, the expected reducible days or events. Posterior means are not the optimal statistic for ranks themselves [26]. Shrunk estimates are steadier year to year and forecast future performance better than raw rates, most of all at low volume [46]. The approach is the one the CMS hospital measures [45] and the COPSS–CMS white paper endorse [23, 21], and reliability is how physician profiling is judged: Adams et al. found most physicians' cost profiles below the 0.7 reliability usually asked of high-stakes use (59% of physicians), with about 22% misclassified in a two-tier network [24]. Hofer et al. made the same point for diabetes report cards [25]. reliability lets an application apply that floor.

    Minimum denominator for a reliability target. In both families B = n/(n + k) on the denominator the model sees, with k = θ₀/τ² on counts and k = s = θ₀(1 − θ₀)/τ² − 1 on proportions. So the denominator at which reliability reaches a target R is

    n_R = k · R/(1 − R)

    and FunnelResult.min_denominator() returns it, multiplied by φ under a scalar within_dispersion so that it is in the caller's units. For a mean this is R/(1 − R) · σ²/τ² cases, with σ² the variance of one case: the classical result for the reliability of a mean. It uses the same θ₀ and τ̂ as the reliability column (the maximum-likelihood τ̂ around the prior's center), so a provider is at or above n_R exactly when its reliability is at or above R; the method reports how many are on each side (n_above, n_below) and the volume they hold. The floor for O/E is in expected events, not cases: it is the expected count that carries the information.

    The range (min_low, min_high) is n_R at the two ends of the profile-likelihood 95% interval for τ (summary["estimate_tau_ci"]). Since n_R ∝ 1/τ², it is wide whenever τ is poorly determined, which is the usual case below about 30 providers, and its upper end is infinite when the interval reaches τ = 0. It reflects the uncertainty in τ only; θ₀ is treated as known. When τ̂ = 0 no floor is returned: with no spread between providers no volume makes a score reliable, and substituting a conventional threshold would misstate that. The target R is not a statistical quantity. 0.7 is the convention for high-stakes profiling [24]; a lower target suits a screen whose results are reviewed, a higher one decisions about a single provider.

    funnel_rollup() repeats this at each level of a hierarchy (physician, group, hospital), fitting each level separately. τ at a coarser level is the spread between that level's units, which is usually smaller than the spread between physicians, so a coarser level's floor is often higher even as its units grow: rolling up helps only when volume grows faster than the floor. The levels are fitted independently, not as one nested model, so the table compares levels; it does not partition the variance between them.

    prob_worse is the posterior probability, under the same model, that the provider's true value is worse than the reference: the upper (or, for worse="lower", lower) tail of Gamma(a + O, b + E) or Beta(α + Y, β + n − Y) at the reference, on effective counts, from the regularized incomplete gamma and beta functions. It is NaN when τ = 0. It answers a different question from signal; §4.16 explains which to use.

    Ranks. The caterpillar plot (plot(kind="caterpillar")) orders providers by eb_rate but draws each interval, because league-table ranks are very uncertain even when real differences between providers exist [47]. Jones and Spiegelhalter [49] set out how screening for divergent providers and estimating each provider's value from the same hierarchical model fit together. Here, signal is the screen and the estimate columns are the estimates.

    At physician level much of the variation in an outcome may belong to the hospital, not the physician. Goodwin et al. found hospitalists varied meaningfully in length of stay and discharge destination but very little in 30-day readmission or mortality, and the hospital explained much of the hospitalist variation [48]. Expect low reliability for physician readmissions, and facet or adjust by hospital or campus.

    Caveat: exchangeability. Shrinking toward θ₀ assumes providers are alike apart from noise and τ². A volume–outcome relationship breaks that, because small providers would then be shrunk toward a mean that is not theirs. The volume test is still planned (§6).

    4.15 Single-case robustness¶

    Physician leaders ask whether a flag is "one patient". With case-level rows, every funnel answers per provider:

    • p_value_drop1: the p-value with the provider's most extreme case removed. That is the case with the largest y − θ₀n toward worse=, or toward the provider's own side when worse=None. It is tested against the same θ₀, τ² and φ. With provider rows from SQL, max_case_y=/max_case_n= supply that case (MAX_BY(obs, obs − exp), with the benchmark 1.0 standing in for θ₀).
    • flag_survives_drop1: for flagged providers, whether they still flag at the alert tier with that case removed. Under signal="fdr" the same p-value cutoff the step used is held fixed.
    • fragility: case rows only. The fewest worst cases that must be removed, in order, before the flag goes (scanning up to min(k − 1, 50)). summary["n_fragile"] counts flagged providers at 1, and a note asks a reviewer to look at that case before acting. This is the provider analogue of a trial's Fragility Index [27], a number clinicians already read.

    No q-value. A request asked for q_value_loo, the leave-one-out p substituted into the Benjamini–Hochberg step. Choosing each provider's worst case is a one-sided selection made after seeing the data, and no false discovery rate guarantee survives it. These columns are descriptive robustness checks, not tests.

    Fragility is not a license to drop cases. Hofer et al. showed that outlier physicians on diabetes report cards could greatly improve their profiles by removing their 1–3 worst-controlled patients [25]. That is exactly why a flag that hinges on one case needs that case reviewed, not removed. Exclusions must be decided by pre-specified rules (§4.12 trimming) before the funnel is drawn, never by fragility after it.

    What fragility means depends on the measure. For days (length of stay), one case contributes many units, and a fragility of 1 says one long stay carries the flag. That is worth reviewing, as a placement delay or a coding error. For event counts (deaths, readmissions) every event is one patient. A low-volume provider flagged on 3 events against 1 expected will almost always have fragility 1, and that is the discreteness of counts, not a data problem. The note says so on event-count charts.

    Trimming comes first. Length-of-stay benchmarks cap or trim outlying stays at DRG-specific points before aggregating (§4.12 advises the same). That is the primary defense against single stays. These columns are a check on what remains. For a model- based check of whole providers, the cross-validated mixed predictive p-values of Marshall and Spiegelhalter [28] remove a provider from the estimate of θ₀ and τ² it is judged against, the circularity of §4.2.

    4.16 Choosing τ¶

    τ, the spread of true values between providers, decides how wide the adjusted funnel is, how much eb_rate shrinks, and what prob_worse means. The model itself, true rates gamma- or beta-distributed around θ₀ with variance τ², is the one the random-effects literature uses: Pavlou et al.'s Normal–Poisson random-effects model [9], drawn exactly. The literature differs on where τ comes from, and qikit offers each:

    The last two rows are from the §5 runs, which plant one outlier in every replicate.

    • On clean data the winsorized estimator and ML agree, and ML is better behaved at small K.
    • ML's advantage does not survive a single outlier. With one gross outlier among 30 providers and no real spread, ML's median τ̂ is 0.216, and that outlier's detection falls from 90% to 67%. With 3% outliers at K = 150, ML overstates τ by two-thirds.
    • The winsorized estimator overstates by a sixth or less.
    • A fixed τ never sees the outliers, so it does not overstate at all.

    Since a screening chart cannot assume its data are free of the outliers it looks for, ML is for data already cleaned, or for comparison with random-effects analyses.

    Why the default is "spiegelhalter". A general-purpose default has to work for a user who does nothing:

    • It needs no history, which rules out a fixed τ as the default.
    • It must not let the providers being screened for widen their own limits. Funnels exist to find outliers, and ML's failure there is the worst failure a screening chart can have. Both winsorized estimators pass.
    • Between the two, the published one errs toward flagging, which is the right error for a screen whose flags are reviewed, and like the CQC, NHS and FunnelPlotR estimators it is uncorrected (§4.3). To reproduce their funnels exactly, use "cqc" or "shmi" (§4.17). Where the stated chance-flag rate matters more than sensitivity, choose "winsorized", and compare the two with funnel_sensitivity().

    Below K = 30 a note recommends a fixed tau=.

    Using a fixed τ well.

    1. Estimate τ on several earlier periods ("winsorized" is the less biased choice), per measure and per facet.
    2. Review those periods' flags and confirm τ is stable from year to year.
    3. Fix τ for the reporting period, and record the value and its source.

    qikit still estimates τ from the current data and reports it as summary["tau_estimated"]. When that is more than 1.5-fold away from the fixed value, a note says the spread may have changed since τ was set. tau=0 gives the unadjusted chart.

    Two questions: signal and prob_worse. Ohlssen, Sharples and Spiegelhalter [35] distinguish divergent providers, unusual against the distribution of peers, from providers whose true value differs from the average. The funnel's signal answers the first: a provider is tested against θ₀ with the spread τ included. CMS's hierarchical models answer the second [23]: a hospital is reported as worse when its shrunk estimate's interval lies above the national rate. prob_worse is that second question in this model, and estimate_flag == "worse" is the same rule read from the interval. The two questions respond to τ in different ways. 10 replicates of K = 500, E ~ U(3, 30), worse="higher", share of providers flagged (scripts/simulate_eb_intervals.py; the estimate columns use the ML prior of §4.14):

    true τ signal (beyond the 95% line) prob_worse > 0.975 estimate_flag worse Truly > 20% above average
    0 1.6% 0.0% 0.0% 0%
    0.15 3.5% 0.1% 0.1% 9.1%
    0.30 4.8% 5.1% 5.1% 23.2%

    As real variation grows, the funnel's flag rate rises only slowly, because that variation is what peers look like. The CMS-style rule starts near zero and climbs faster, because more providers really are worse than average. Use signal to choose whom to review as unusual. Use prob_worse when the question is whether a provider is probably worse than average, for example for improvement targeting, and say which question was asked.

    State of the literature. No healthcare publication found applies, recommends or evaluates the winsorization consistency factor. Funnel guidelines [8], applied studies, FunnelPlotR [13] and the NHS indicators use the uncorrected estimate, and one applied paper states that it averages 1 under the null, which winsorizing makes untrue. The default rests on eq. 9 and qikit's own simulations, not on external validation, so it is an option, not the default. A report that compares qikit with a published funnel uses the default, or "cqc"/"shmi" for a CQC or SHMI funnel (§4.17). The random-effects literature [9, 23, 35] prefers likelihood estimation or a pre-specified τ. qikit offers both, with the robustness caveat above.

    4.17 CQC and SHMI: over-dispersion on a transformed scale¶

    The Care Quality Commission [36] and the NHS Summary Hospital-level Mortality Indicator [37] measure over-dispersion on a scale where the null variance does not depend on the center, and draw normal limits there. FunnelPlotR [13] implements both (sr_method = "CQC" and "SHMI"). tau_method="cqc" and "shmi" follow its source:

    "cqc" "shmi"
    Scale T √ for ratios and rates; arcsine √ for proportions log, for ratios and rates only
    SD under the null, sᵢ 1/(2√nᵢ) 1/√(θ₀nᵢ)
    Robustness z winsorized at its 10th and 90th percentiles providers in the lowest and highest tenths of ranked z dropped (rank groups ⌊rank·10/(K + 1)⌋, keep 1–8)
    φ̂, τ̂² mean(z²); DerSimonian–Laird on wᵢ = 1/sᵢ², 0 when Kφ̂ < K − 1 same
    Limits T⁻¹(T(θ₀) ± z·√(sᵢ² + τ̂²)) same

    summary["tau_scale"] records the scale and summary["limit_method"] says "transformed_normal". P-values come from the same normal model on the same scale, so a point beyond the line still has p below the line's level. With τ̂² = 0 both methods draw the exact unadjusted contours, the same as every other method, since the transformed normal would only approximate them. The shrinkage columns use τ̂² carried back to the y scale by the delta method (4θ₀τ² for √, 4θ₀(1 − θ₀)τ² for arcsine, θ₀²τ² for log).

    Checked against FunnelPlotR. tests/test_reference.py compares φ̂, τ̂² and the limits with FunnelPlotR on its medpar data (standardized ratios under both methods, length of stay, and a proportion) to 1e-6. Two departures are deliberate:

    • FunnelPlotR draws CQC ratio limits on the √ scale. It returns 1 ± z·√(s² + τ²) without squaring back, so its adjusted CQC limits for a ratio are about half as wide as the method intends. qikit squares them back. φ̂ and τ̂² agree exactly; only the limits are skipped in the reference test.
    • Arcsine limits past 100%. Where the upper arcsine contour passes π/2, sin² folds back below 1 in FunnelPlotR. qikit caps the limit at 1, the largest proportion possible. The reference test masks those points.

    A provider with no events has z = −∞ on the log scale. When truncation keeps it, FunnelPlotR returns φ̂ = ∞; qikit leaves it out of φ̂.

    When to use them. To reproduce a CQC or SHMI funnel, or to report on the same basis as those indicators. For a physician screen the default is preferred: its limits are exact rather than normal on a transformed scale, which matters at the small expected counts most physicians have. funnel_sensitivity() shows which flags change.

    4.18 Benchmarks: what the best providers achieve (benchmark=)¶

    A funnel shows which providers differ from the center line. The center line is the average, and the average is not a goal. benchmark= adds a goal that the best providers in the same data already reach. It is drawn as a dashed line, and it does not change signal. Both options need worse=. benchmark_share= (default 0.10) defines "the best".

    benchmark= What it is Suits
    "abc" Kiefe and Weissman's achievable benchmark of care [38, 39]: the pooled rate of the best providers holding 10% of patients Process-of-care proportions (H&P in 24 h, vaccination)
    "eb" The 90th percentile, toward better, of the shrunk estimates eb_rate [44] Outcomes (O/E mortality, length of stay, readmission) and per-case amounts (measure="mean": minutes or cost per case)

    Both run on every measure. "abc" on "oe", "rate" or "mean" adds a note that its authors designed it for proportions; "eb" is the choice there.

    The ABC method. These are the three steps of Kiefe et al. [38, Table 1], restated by Weissman et al. [39, Table 1]:

    1. Rank providers best first by the adjusted performance fraction, APF = (x + 1)/(d + 2). The authors give it as a Bayesian estimator, citing Agresti. It is the posterior mean under a uniform prior, and it keeps a provider with 3 of 3 from topping the list on luck: that provider's APF is 0.8, below 196 of 200 at 0.975. The APF is "used solely for the purpose of ranking". In no event should it be used to compare individual providers [39, p. 272].
    2. Starting with the best provider, add providers until together they hold at least 10% of all patients. The 10% is of patients, not providers, so a run of tiny providers cannot make up the group. The point where the group reaches 10% is the breakpoint. Every provider tied at the breakpoint joins, so the share held can run past 10%, to 12% "or even higher" [39, p. 273]. qikit reports it as coverage in summary["benchmark"].
    3. The ABC is the group's patients who received the intervention divided by all of the group's patients: the pooled raw rate Σx/Σd. It is not the mean of their rates and not their APFs. Some providers in the group can fall below the ABC itself.

    worse= sets the direction. With worse="lower", a high value is good and providers are ranked from the highest APF down. With worse="higher", as for low-value care [42], they are ranked from the lowest up. Excluded providers neither join the group nor count toward the share.

    Worked examples checked in tests/test_funnel.py: - GAMUT [41]. Delta (196/200), Envoy (193/200) and SkyWest (125/130) hold 530 of 5,237 patients (10.1%), so the ABC is 514/530 = 0.970. - Kiefe 1998, smoking-cessation counselling [38]. Across 106 hospitals and 1,253 patients the pooled rate was 14%. The 12 best hospitals held at least 125 patients and gave an ABC of 49%. The source data are not published, so this one is not a test.

    At physician level. In the randomized trial [40], 70 physicians, each with at least 20 eligible patients, were ranked on the APF until 10% of patients were included. The ABCs for five diabetes process measures ranged from 82% to 99%. Adding the ABC to feedback raised the odds of appropriate care by 1.33 to 1.57 times.

    The share. Weissman et al. call 10% "somewhat arbitrary". Another share may be used if it is agreed before the benchmark providers are identified [39, p. 273]. Hall et al. [43] used 15%, to include more patients and hospitals and to reduce the influence of small ones. Set benchmark_share= in advance, never after seeing who would be in the group.

    Minimum sufficient denominator (MSD). Kiefe et al. [38, Table 3] tabulate, for each mean rate p̄, the fewest patients at which 100% performance would differ significantly from p̄. They give no formula. The smallest N with p̄ᴺ < 0.025 reproduces all 19 rows of their table, so the rule is: N of N treated must be a one-sided exact binomial event at 2.5% (two-sided 5%). That is a reconstruction, not the authors' stated formula. The nine MSDs Hall et al. report [43, Table 4] match the same table read at the overall rate rounded down to 5%. At a 94% rate they used the 90% row (36), where the exact rule gives 60.

    qikit ranks every provider on the APF, as Kiefe and Weissman do, and reports the MSD in summary["benchmark"] without using it to rank: - msd: the exact rule, at the center line in the good direction (1 − p̄ when lower is better). - n_below_msd: how many eligible providers fall below it. - without_small: the ABC recomputed without those providers. - A note when that changes the ABC by more than 5%.

    Hall et al. used the MSD as a switch, with the APF only below it and the raw rate above. They found their warfarin benchmark fell from 94% to 76% when hospitals below the MSD were excluded. without_small is that check. For counts with worse="higher", the MSD is the analogue for zero events: the expected count at which P(0) = e^(−θ₀n) < 0.025, so n > 3.69/θ₀.

    Reporting. Following Hall et al. [43], summary["benchmark"] holds method, share and value, and under "abc" also: - providers: how many providers set the ABC. - coverage: their share of the patients. - group_denominator: the median and range of those providers' patients. - msd, n_below_msd and without_small, as above.

    The meets_benchmark column marks whether each provider is at the benchmark or better. Hall's "top performers" are those at or above it.

    A note calls the ABC provisional when fewer than three providers set it.

    O/E and rates under "abc": an extension, against the authors' advice. The ABC was designed for process-of-care indicators. Weissman et al. "urge that it be applied to outcome measures with only extreme caution, if at all" [39, p. 271]. Outcomes need risk adjustment, are less sensitive to quality, and can be gamed through coding [39, pp. 274–275]. They also oppose the ABC where "the right rate is not known, particularly when the purpose is to decrease use of medical services" [39, p. 274], which covers length of stay and low-value care.

    qikit still computes it for "oe" and "rate". It ranks by (y + 1)/(n + 1/θ₀), the same posterior-mean idea with a prior worth one event at the center line; for O/E with θ₀ = 1 that is (O + 1)/(E + 1). Under within_dispersion, ranking uses y/φ out of n/φ. Each such result carries a note pointing to "eb".

    "eb": the shrunk-percentile benchmark. This is the (1 − benchmark_share) quantile of eb_rate across eligible providers, toward the better side: the 10th percentile of O/E when higher is worse. It uses the posterior means of the funnel's own random-effects model (§4.14). O'Brien et al. [44] proposed it, and Hall et al. [43] compared it with the ABC. The two were within 3% for most indicators, but the shrunk version was 7–32% lower where small providers dominated, because shrinkage pulls their lucky extremes back. It needs no adjustment for small numbers, it is defined for any measure the funnel fits, and it suits outcomes. When the funnel finds no spread (τ̂ = 0, or overdispersion=False) every shrunk estimate is the center line, and so is the benchmark; a note says so. Hall et al. kept the ABC for its transparency. "eb" is less transparent, and better founded for outcomes.

    The percentile counts providers, as O'Brien et al. do, not patients as the ABC does. At physician level, many low-volume physicians are shrunk close to the center line, which pulls the percentile toward it. A patient-weighted percentile would match the ABC's "10% of patients", but it is not the published method.

    When the two disagree. On an outcome, report "eb". An ABC far better than the "eb" benchmark is pooling providers who were lucky. On the bundled surgeon data, with τ̂ = 0, the ABC is an O/E of 0.61 and "eb" is the center line, 1.02. There is no spread the data can show, so no surgeon is reliably better than average.

    Peer groups. Hall et al. found that benchmarks from regional stroke centers differed from those of all hospitals. Larger centers face different case mix and constraints [43]. facets= gives each peer group its own benchmark, for example facets="hospital_type". Choose peer groups for comparability before looking at the benchmarks.

    Intended use. The authors present the ABC in confidential, non-punitive feedback. They discourage its use for physician compensation, credentialing or public report cards, where statistical confidence matters more [39, p. 274]. Hall et al. report no confidence interval around the ABC, "because the purpose of our benchmarks is to drive quality improvement". The same holds for "eb". A benchmark is a goal, not a test. At physician volumes the gap between a provider and the benchmark is often within chance, and min_detectable and ci_lower/ci_upper say how far the provider's own data can tell.

    4.19 Continuous per-case amounts (measure="mean")¶

    Minutes of EHR time per discharge, cost per case and days per stay are amounts, not counts. The question is the same: does a provider's mean differ from its peers' by more than chance and the spread between providers allow?

    The model. measure="mean" is the quasi-Poisson model of §4.12 applied to the raw amounts: y = the amount, n = 1 per case, and φ estimated. A provider's total over k cases has variance φ·θⱼ·k, so its mean has variance φθⱼ/k. φ is the variance-to-mean ratio of one case, in y's units (minutes²/minute), and summary["case_sd"] = √(φ̂·θ₀) reports the within-provider SD of one case in plain units. τ² is added on top as for every measure (§4.4), and the center line is the pooled mean Σy/Σk.

    Why no rescaling is needed. The model is scale-equivariant. Multiply every amount by c and φ̂ is multiplied by c, the effective counts y/φ and k/φ are unchanged, and so are every p-value, q-value, reliability and flag. Only the limits, the center line and the amounts move, by c. Minutes and hours therefore give the same chart, labeled differently, and the test suite checks this. It is also why the construction users built first agrees with it to machine precision: dividing each case by the pooled mean and passing it as O/E with n = 1, then multiply= the pooled mean. That is the same model at c = 1/θ₀.

    Why not a normal model. A normal model with one σ² for every provider would add a new family to every part of the engine: contours, tests, τ estimators, shrinkage, power, the benchmarks. Variance proportional to the mean fits non-negative, right-skewed amounts at least as well, because providers with longer times usually vary more, and every option comes along unchanged: signal="fdr", tau_method, drop-one robustness, benchmark="eb", facets= and multiply=.

    Inputs. - Case rows: y= the amount, one row per case. n= may be omitted; if given, it must be 1. - SQL totals: y = SUM(amount), n = COUNT(*) and sum_sq = SUM(amount * amount). This is the Σ y²/n of §4.12 with n = 1. cases= is implied by n, and max_case_y= alone gives drop-one robustness.

    within_dispersion is always estimated, or given per provider as a column. A scalar is refused, because no one knows φ in minutes²/minute. fdr_min_denominator counts cases. The "φ below 1" note of §4.12 does not apply: φ < 1 here means only that the amounts are in small units.

    Limits. - Amounts must be ≥ 0. Signed values, such as change scores, are refused (§6). - Long cases dominate φ̂, as long stays do. Cap amounts at a stated percentile first where the measure's convention does.

    6. Known limitations and planned work¶

    • Small cohorts. τ̂ is imprecise below about 30 providers (IQR 0.17–0.27 when τ = 0.3, K = 30), and the pooled center line can be pulled by one extreme provider (§4.2).
    • Debiasing at small K. The consistency factor of tau_method="winsorized" (§4.3) assumes residuals near normal. With few providers and no real spread it overstates τ slightly (median 0.06–0.08 at K = 30, τ = 0). A note says so below K = 30. A pre-specified tau= is the remedy there (§4.16). tau_method="ml" is better only on clean data.
    • Maximum likelihood is not robust. tau_method="ml" has no winsorizing. One gross outlier among 30 providers takes its median τ̂ from 0 to 0.22, and 3% outliers at K = 150 inflate it by two-thirds (§4.16, §5).
    • No outcome–volume test yet [1, §4; 8, step 5]. A funnel assumes outcome does not depend on volume.
    • Binary outcomes on an O/E chart. Observed counts that are sums of yes/no outcomes have variance Σp(1 − p) < Σp = E. Poisson limits are therefore slightly too wide for common outcomes: about 8% at a 15% event rate, negligible for mortality. within_dispersion="estimate" from case rows finds φ̂ ≈ 1 − θ̄ and corrects it; the summary labels this as the binomial-variance correction rather than flooring it at 1 (§4.12). At small counts its fractional effective counts make the test interpolated rather than exact. With a scalar within_dispersion the default stays 1.
    • Within-provider clustering. One φ per chart (or facet), or one φᵢ per provider from a column the caller computes (§4.12). qikit does not yet estimate case-mix φ_g from stay rows itself.
    • Items clustered in surveys use a single pooled φ (§4.13). A provider whose patients skip items has smaller clusters and a slightly smaller design effect, 1 + (m̄ⱼ − 1)ρ. qikit does not model this; it is negligible when item nonresponse is low.
    • Item-level rows hide the clustering. qikit cannot tell items from cases when every row is one yes/no. It refuses "estimate" on such rows and documents the unit to pass (§4.13).
    • Signed continuous values. measure="mean" needs amounts ≥ 0, because its variance grows with the mean (§4.19). Change scores and other signed values would need a normal family with a common σ², which is not built.
    • Very large expected counts. Above λ = 10⁵ the contours and p-values switch to Byar's approximation, and summary["limit_method"] says "byar". No physician-level measure comes near this.
    • Planned: a robust likelihood estimate of τ (heavy-tailed random effects, or detect-then-refit [35]), the volume test, strata= (per-DRG φ_g from stay rows, shrunk toward the pool [19], combined to φᵢ), and a discrete false discovery rate step for bounded tails [31, 32].
    • Evaluated and declined (so they are not proposed again without new evidence):
    • Per-provider own-variance O/E with limma-style shrinkage (§4.12): self-masking.
    • q_value from leave-one-out p-values (§4.15): post-selection.
    • Storey's π̂₀ and a power-screened BH (§4.11): little gain with τ² in the null, and the screen drops gross low-volume outliers.
    • A winsorized or median center line. At K in the hundreds no provider moves ΣO/ΣE. The median of ratios is not volume-weighted and is skewed at small E. A center other than ΣO/ΣE breaks Σ excess = 0. center=1.0 is already the robust anchor (§4.2).
    • Monotone "smoothed" display contours. No sawtooth was found on the drawn grid in any family or volume range. The proposed running minimum on upper limits would draw lines inside the exact test, showing unflagged points beyond a line. If ever needed, the conservative envelope u(n) = max over m ≥ n of u(m) is the correct form.
    • scipy special functions in dist.py (§4.5).
